How to use the result

The tool identifies a covered question, checks the reported circumstances, and links to the relevant recommendation. Each result shows the information used and any details still needed.

“Outside current coverage” means the tool cannot assess that topic. It does not mean CBCT is inappropriate. A supported result is guidance for consideration, not a patient-specific prescription.

Topics currently covered

Selected endodontic questions: unclear tooth symptoms, persistent disease, suspected fractures, root resorption, canal anatomy, retreatment, root-end surgery and possible endodontic sinusitis. Also presurgical implant planning, localized dental trauma, impacted-tooth localization and third-molar anatomical risk.

Routine screening and sleep-apnea diagnostic requests receive purpose-specific notices. General TMJ, jaw pathology, orthodontic, periodontal and implant follow-up or complication pathways are not yet included.

Estimate scan volume

Estimate your monthly CBCT scans.

Start with your monthly cases. See your scan total, how study findings relate to those cases, and optional revenue from scans alone.

1. Enter your monthly cases

Include only cases you have assessed as appropriate for CBCT. We assume each entered case proceeds to one scan. Count a scan once under its main reason, even if several categories apply. Leave other fields at zero or blank.

“Which cases count?” gives examples within each category; the symptoms alone do not establish a need for CBCT.

Which cases count?

Pain that is difficult to locate, conflicting clinical findings, or symptoms involving more than one tooth, when the examination and 2D images do not answer the endodontic question.

Which cases count?

Persistent symptoms, a sinus tract or a nonhealing lesion after root canal treatment, when further information is needed to decide on care. Excludes routine follow-up of a tooth that is healing as expected.

Which cases count?

A suspected root fracture with findings such as isolated deep probing, persistent biting pain or a suspicious bone-loss pattern, when the examination and 2D images are inconclusive. CBCT cannot reliably rule out every fracture.

Which cases count?

Internal, external or cervical resorption requiring assessment of location, extent or treatment options beyond what the examination and 2D images provide.

Which cases count?

A suspected missed canal, complex anatomy, separated instrument, perforation or other complication where 3D information is needed for retreatment planning. If already counted under nonhealing, do not count the same scan again.

Which cases count?

Planning apical or root-end surgery, including root position, lesion extent and relationships to the sinus, nerve canal, cortical bone or nearby roots.

Which cases count?

Presurgical implant planning: ridge width and height, nearby nerves and sinus, and assessment of grafting needs. Count a scan once even if it covers more than one proposed implant site.

Which cases count?

A suspected dental source of sinus disease or an upper tooth infection whose relationship to the sinus needs clarification after initial assessment. Incidental sinus thickening alone is not an automatic indication.

Which cases count?

An impacted or ectopic tooth with an unresolved question about 3D position, adjacent roots, resorption or surgical risk that could affect management. Excludes routine scanning of every impacted tooth.

Which cases count?

Cases outside these categories for which you have already established a specific CBCT indication. They contribute to scan volume and revenue only; no decision-change percentage is assigned.

Scan selection

Field of view & image detail

Choose the scan size and resolution that match the diagnostic question, patient context, and level of detail needed.

On this pageScan coverageImage detailSystem & lab notes

Main principle

Match the scan to the question

Select the scan coverage and image detail needed to answer the clinical question, considering the individual patient and ALADA/ALADAIP principles. Avoid unnecessary exposure; the smallest scan or highest resolution is not always the appropriate choice.

Localized questionSmaller fields of view are often appropriate when the clinical question is localized.
Broader anatomyBroader coverage may be appropriate when the anatomy of interest extends beyond one focused region.
Fine detailHigher resolution may be useful when subtle endodontic, root-surface, or cortical detail is clinically relevant.
Larger structuresStandard or lower-dose protocols may fit broader anatomy when fine-detail visualization is not the primary objective.

Scan coverage

Compare the area each field of view covers. Expand a card to review examples.

Focus field

Useful when the clinical question is highly localized and fine detail is important.

Review examples

May fit questions such as

  • Localized endodontic concern
  • Suspected vertical root fracture
  • Localized resorption evaluation
  • Focused periapical assessment
  • High-detail review of a limited anatomical region
  • Localized pre-surgical endodontic assessment

Single jaw

A single-jaw scan may be the practical minimum when implant planning, intraoral scan integration, or guide design needs continuous anatomy across one arch.

Review examples

Worth considering when

  • Multiple teeth within one arch are being evaluated
  • Implant planning requires regional anatomical context
  • Intraoral scan overlay or surgical guide design is anticipated
  • The anatomy of interest extends beyond a focus field
  • Sinus or mandibular canal relationship must be evaluated within one arch

Dual jaw

Dual-jaw imaging may help preserve anatomical relationship between arches while avoiding unnecessary expansion into a broader cranial scan.

Review examples

May be useful when

  • Both arches require evaluation together
  • Occlusal or opposing-tooth relationships are clinically relevant
  • Symptoms are difficult to localize between arches
  • Upper and lower anatomy together may improve interpretation
  • Broader restorative or functional relationships must be visualized

5x8 regional scan

A 5x8 FOV refers to approximately 5 cm width by 8 cm height, allowing upper and lower arch anatomy to be captured within a focused regional scan.

A 5x8 regional scan may be useful when the clinician needs focused anatomical detail while still capturing vertically related upper and lower anatomy within one localized region.

Review examples

Examples

  • Pain that radiates between upper and lower arches
  • Symptoms that are difficult to localize clinically
  • Evaluation involving opposing posterior teeth
  • Localized regional pathology involving both arches
  • A traditional focus field may be too restrictive vertically
  • Regional implant or surgical evaluation involving opposing anatomy

A 5x8 scan may help keep the view focused while still showing upper and lower anatomy in the same region.

Cranial scan

Cranial scans are generally reserved for situations where the clinical question cannot reasonably be answered using a more limited field of view.

Review examples

May be considered when

  • Airway or broader craniofacial anatomy is relevant
  • Orthodontic evaluation requires broad relationships
  • Broader surgical planning is being evaluated
  • Trauma or larger pathology assessment requires larger coverage
  • Bilateral or multi-regional anatomy must be reviewed together
  • Full craniofacial relationships are clinically relevant

Image detail

High resolution

May be useful when fine detail may affect interpretation.

Review examples
  • Fine endodontic detail
  • Suspected vertical root fracture
  • Resorption detail
  • Subtle periapical findings
  • Root morphology or canal anatomy
  • Pre-surgical endodontic planning

Standard resolution

May be appropriate when the diagnostic question is not dependent on the smallest visible detail.

Review examples
  • Broader anatomy review
  • Implant planning that does not require ultra-fine endodontic detail
  • Sinus, airway, impaction, orthodontic, or larger structural questions

Lower-dose / lower-resolution protocols

Often fit questions involving larger structures rather than subtle fine-detail interpretation.

Review examples
  • Larger anatomical structures
  • Broader anatomical orientation
  • Airway, sinus, orthodontic, craniofacial, or structural evaluation
  • Patient-specific dose considerations

System & lab notes

These notes refer to CSD systems and laboratory workflows. Confirm requirements for your equipment and lab.

High-resolution coverage on CSD systems

Some CBCT systems restrict high-resolution imaging to very small focus fields. CSD CBCT systems can capture high-resolution datasets across field sizes larger than a traditional localized focus field. This may allow clinicians to see fine detail while still including enough surrounding anatomy to answer the clinical question.

Preparing scans for laboratory workflows

High-resolution datasets from CSD CBCT systems may contain more detail and larger files than some labs prefer for routine guide fabrication or digital workflows.

Many labs prioritize manageable file size, enough anatomical coverage, efficient workflow, and easier segmentation rather than maximum possible resolution.

A scan optimized for fine clinical review may not always be the ideal dataset for every laboratory workflow. Confirm preferred scan settings, export requirements, file formats, and workflow limits before sending datasets.

This guidance is educational and intended to help align scan selection with the clinical question and ALADA/ALADAIP radiation-safety principles. It does not diagnose, recommend treatment, replace clinician judgment, or determine the correct scan protocol for an individual patient.

Image review

Review images in CS 3D Imaging.

Start with the basic review steps, then choose the area you want to examine. Review the full scan as well as the area of concern.

Before reviewing a specific area

  1. Use the slice views (MPR) first, not the 3D rendering, for image review and measurements.
  2. Orient the area of interest so the tooth or ridge is straight in at least two planes.
  3. Scroll slowly through adjacent slices before deciding whether a finding is real.
  4. Save screenshots that show the finding in more than one plane.

Choose a review topic

Tooth and sinus relationship → Resorption → Cracks and fractures → Root canals and endodontic anatomy → Implant site and surgical anatomy → Periodontal and bone defects →
Tooth and sinus relationship
  1. Follow the root apices, sinus floor, and adjacent mucosa in sagittal and coronal views.
  2. Look for a dental source plus a matching sinus response, not mucosal thickening alone.
  3. Ask whether the cortical sinus floor is intact, thinned, or interrupted.
  4. When the relationship is uncertain, describe the uncertainty rather than calling it dental in origin too quickly.
Back to review topics ↑
Resorption
  1. Center the tooth and scroll from crown to apex in axial slices.
  2. Decide whether the defect starts inside the canal space or on the external root surface.
  3. Describe location, depth, pulpal proximity, and bone communication.
  4. Use the scan as an anatomic map, then confirm significance with clinical findings.
Back to review topics ↑
Cracks and fractures
  1. Do not require a visible crack line; it may be hidden by voxel size or artifact.
  2. Look for supporting bone patterns: isolated narrow defects, halo loss, or buccal/lingual breakout.
  3. Be cautious near posts, crowns, and root filling material.
  4. Interpret CBCT together with probing, bite testing, symptoms, and 2D images before any clinical decision.
Back to review topics ↑
Root canals and endodontic anatomy
  1. Start at the chamber floor and follow canal paths apically in axial slices.
  2. Use the synchronized crosshair to confirm the same structure in all three views.
  3. Map missed anatomy, curvature, calcification, perforation risk, and apical anatomy.
  4. Keep the scan question specific and document what clinical uncertainty the image is helping clarify.
Back to review topics ↑
Implant site and surgical anatomy
  1. Trace the arch and evaluate cross-sectional slices at the proposed site.
  2. Measure ridge width, height, concavities, sinus or nasal floor, and nerve position in cross-section.
  3. Keep the restorative goal, anatomy, and patient-specific risk factors in the same view.
  4. Record the slice and plane used for key measurements.
Back to review topics ↑
Periodontal and bone defects
  1. Evaluate buccal, lingual, mesial, and distal surfaces separately.
  2. Differentiate periodontal defects from endodontic lesions by tracking their origin and extent.
  3. Thin bone may disappear because of artifact or resolution limits, so be cautious before calling dehiscence.
  4. Use CBCT as an anatomic map, then confirm with probing and clinical attachment findings.
Back to review topics ↑

Review the full scan

  1. Review beyond the immediate tooth: sinuses, nasal cavity, canals, cortices, condyles, and airway space.
  2. Separate dental findings from incidental or nonodontogenic findings in the chart.
  3. Refer when a finding is outside the clinical question or your interpretive comfort.
  4. Document what was reviewed and why the scan was taken.

Check for patient movement first. Motion can duplicate borders, blur fine anatomy, and change the apparent size of small defects. Review the whole scan for motion before interpreting subtle findings.

About & references

How the case tool works

The case tool recognizes selected clinical questions, checks the reported circumstances and shows guidance with source links. It distinguishes missing information, conflicting details, existing imaging, unsupported purposes and topics outside its coverage.

Descriptions and follow-up answers are processed in your browser; they are not sent or saved. Omit patient identifiers. Recognition can be incomplete, and the software has not been independently validated as a clinical decision instrument.

Each result explains which information was used. It is not a diagnosis, a probability of disease or a prescription for a scan. Read the decision rules, source map and limitations.

The scan estimator uses your entered case counts and optional fee. These values and study outcome percentages do not influence clinical guidance.
